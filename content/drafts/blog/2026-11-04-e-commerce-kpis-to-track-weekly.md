# E-commerce KPIs to Track Weekly: What a Small Store Actually Needs

Most small stores do not have a data problem. They have a "too many numbers" problem. Your dashboard shows forty charts, and none of them tells you what to do on Monday morning. This guide lists the e-commerce KPIs to track weekly, why each one earns its place, and what a bad week looks like.

You don't need a data team to run this review. You need one short list, read at the same time every week, with the same comparison each time. Set aside twenty minutes and a coffee, and you will catch problems while they are still small.

## Why weekly is the right rhythm

Daily numbers are noisy. A single slow Tuesday tells you almost nothing, and checking every morning invites panic over random swings. Monthly reviews have the opposite flaw: by the time you see a drop, you have already lost four weeks of sales.

Weekly sits in the middle. [Shero Commerce](https://sherocommerce.com/shopify-top-kpis/) makes the same argument, calling weekly the best balance between monthly reviews that are too slow and daily figures that are too noisy. A full week also smooths out the weekend effect, so Monday-to-Sunday comparisons are fair.

One rule keeps the habit honest. Compare each week to the week before it, not to the same week last year. Your store has changed too much in twelve months for the older number to be a useful yardstick.

Before the list, it helps to see how the pieces fit together. [Kanal's guide](https://getkanal.com/blog/ecommerce-kpis) sums it up in one line: revenue equals sessions multiplied by conversion rate multiplied by average order value. Their worked example uses 20,000 sessions at a 2.5% conversion rate, which gives 500 orders, and 500 orders at $60 gives $30,000.

This matters because it tells you where to look when revenue moves. If revenue fell, one of three things happened. Fewer people arrived, fewer of them bought, or each buyer spent less.

Every KPI in the next section is a closer look at one of those three levers. That is why a short list is enough. The e-commerce KPIs to track weekly are really a way to find which lever moved.

## The weekly KPIs worth your twenty minutes

Here are the numbers that earn a spot on a small store's weekly review. Keep them in one spreadsheet or one saved report so they never take more than a few minutes.

**1. Revenue, compared with last week.** Start here, because it is the headline. Write down the total and the percent change, then move on to the numbers that explain it.

**2. Total orders.** Orders are less affected by price changes than revenue is. [Shero Commerce](https://sherocommerce.com/shopify-top-kpis/) suggests that a week-over-week drop of 20% or more calls for immediate investigation, while a dip of 5 to 10% can be normal.

**3. Sessions by channel.** Total traffic hides the story.

Split it into search, social, email, paid ads and direct, because a change in the mix often explains a change in conversion. A flood of cheap social visits can lower your conversion rate without anything being wrong on your site.

**4. Conversion rate.** This is the share of sessions that end in a purchase. [Shopaccino](https://www.shopaccino.com/blog/ecommerce-dashboard-metrics-every-founder-should-track-weekly) lists it among the metrics every founder should check weekly, and for good reason: a sudden drop usually points to a broken checkout, a pricing change or a slow page.

**5. Average order value.** This is revenue divided by orders. It moves slowly, so you are looking for a sharp change after a promotion or a new bundle, not a daily trend.

**6. Cart abandonment rate.** It shows how many shoppers add something and leave. If it jumps, check shipping costs shown at checkout and any new steps you added.

**7. Return on ad spend.** If you run paid ads, divide attributed revenue by ad spend, as [Kanal](https://getkanal.com/blog/ecommerce-kpis) defines it. Read it next to margin, because a healthy ratio on thin-margin products can still lose money.

**8. Returning customer rate.** This is returning customers divided by all customers. [Kanal](https://getkanal.com/blog/ecommerce-kpis) notes that Shopify's own reference says most stores display 20 to 40%, so treat that as a sanity check and not a target.

**9. Fulfilment time.** Slow shipping hurts later, not now. [Shero Commerce](https://sherocommerce.com/shopify-top-kpis/) links shipping that takes more than 48 hours to a retention drop two to three weeks afterward.

## How to read the numbers together

A single KPI rarely gives you the answer. The value comes from reading two or three side by side.

Suppose revenue is down 12% and orders are down 11%. Average order value barely moved, so the problem is the number of buyers.

Next, check sessions by channel. If paid traffic fell, your ad budget or approval is the cause. If traffic held steady but conversion dropped, look at the site itself.

Now suppose revenue is flat but profit feels worse. Check return on ad spend and shipping costs. Often a store sells the same amount while paying more to acquire each order.

This is the habit that turns the e-commerce KPIs to track weekly into decisions. For each number, ask one question: what changed, and what is the most likely reason? Write your answer in one sentence, then pick one action for the week.

## What to leave out of the weekly review

Some metrics are real but belong on a slower clock than the e-commerce KPIs to track weekly. Customer lifetime value needs months of data to mean anything. [Growth Suite's expert answer](https://www.growthsuite.net/questions/what-key-performance-indicators-should-i-check-weekly-vs-monthly) is a useful read on splitting weekly and monthly checks, and many sources suggest reviewing average order value and lifetime value on a monthly or quarterly cycle.

Vanity numbers also deserve a pass. Social followers, page views and email list size feel good, but they rarely change what you do on Monday. If a number never leads to an action, remove it from the sheet.

Keep your list of e-commerce KPIs to track weekly short on purpose. Nine numbers fit on one screen, and a review you actually finish beats a perfect one you skip.

## A simple weekly routine

Pick a fixed slot, such as Monday at 9am, and follow the same five steps each time. This routine is the whole practice of the e-commerce KPIs to track weekly, and it fits in one sitting.

1. Open your saved report and copy the nine numbers into your sheet.
2. Add the change versus last week for each one.
3. Flag anything that moved by more than 10%, using your own judgment as a store owner.
4. Write one sentence on the most likely cause of each flag.
5. Choose one action and one owner for the week.

Over a few months your sheet becomes a record of what normal looks like for your store. That record is worth more than any benchmark, because your products, prices and customers are yours alone. ## FAQ

### How many KPIs should a small store track each week?

Aim for seven to ten. Fewer than five leaves blind spots, and more than ten turns the review into a chore. The e-commerce KPIs to track weekly in this guide are a good starting set, and you can swap one or two to suit your business.

### What is a good conversion rate for an online store?

It depends on your product, price and traffic source. Published averages are often quoted at two to three percent, but your own trend matters more than any average. Track your rate for a month, then judge each week against it.

### Should I look at KPIs daily instead?

Only for specific events, such as a big sale or a site change. For routine decisions, daily figures are too noisy, and weekly reviews give you a calmer and more reliable signal.

### Which tool do I need?

Your store platform's built-in reports are enough to start. A spreadsheet that holds the nine numbers and the week-over-week change covers the rest.

### What if I do not have enough traffic for weekly numbers to mean anything?

Low-volume stores see more random swings. In that case, review weekly but compare a four-week average, and treat small changes as noise.
