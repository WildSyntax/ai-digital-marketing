# How to Find Which Products Are Losing Money in One Hour

Last updated: 4 November 2026

Most small stores can't say which products are losing money, because the report they check every week ranks products by revenue. Revenue shows what sells. To see what pays for the business, you need each product's contribution margin: the price minus every cost that grows with each order. This guide shows how to build that view in a spreadsheet in about an hour, without a data team or paid tools.

## Why revenue reports hide which products are losing money

A revenue report counts the money that comes in and ignores what each order costs to fulfil. A best seller can need heavy ad spend, expensive shipping and frequent refunds. Once those costs are subtracted, it can leave less per order than a quiet product, or lose money on every sale.

Store-wide averages make this worse. When you look at margin for the whole store, a few strong products cover for the weak ones. Conjura's [guide to contribution margin](https://www.conjura.com/blog/the-ultimate-guide-to-contribution-margin-for-ecommerce-brands) describes a fashion retailer that found about 40% of its ad budget going to products with a negative contribution margin. Moving that budget to profitable products raised net profit by about 22% within three months, according to the same vendor case study.

## What counts as a variable cost

Contribution margin is revenue minus the costs that rise and fall with each order. Rent, salaries and software subscriptions stay out, because they do not change when you sell one more unit. Everything you pay because an order happened goes in, including ads that were needed to win it.

| Cost | Where to find it |
|---|---|
| Product cost, landed (freight and duties included) | supplier invoices |
| Packaging | supplier invoices |
| Shipping to the customer | shipping app or carrier invoices |
| Payment processing fees | payment provider report |
| Marketplace or platform fees | marketplace statements |
| Returns and refunds | store refunds report |
| Ad spend for the product | ad platform, by campaign or product |

Use landed cost rather than the purchase price. Freight and import duties are part of what each unit costs you. Leaving them out makes every product look better than it is.

## How to calculate product margin in a spreadsheet

To calculate product margin for each item, export one month of orders, add every variable cost per order, and subtract the total from revenue. Divide by units sold to get contribution per unit. Then sort products by total contribution in dollars. For a store with under 100 products, the first pass takes about an hour.

1. Export last month's orders with one row per product per order: product name, units, price, discounts and refunds.
2. Add a column for landed product cost per unit, taken from your supplier invoices.
3. Add shipping and packaging per order. If an order holds several products, split the shipping cost by each product's share of the order value.
4. Add payment and platform fees. Most providers charge a percentage plus a fixed fee per transaction, so use the rates on your statement.
5. Add ad spend per product. If your campaigns are not split by product, divide each campaign's spend by the revenue share of the products it promotes.
6. Subtract all costs from net revenue to get contribution per product, then divide by units for contribution per unit.
7. Sort by total contribution in dollars, largest first.

Step 3 matters more than it looks. Practical Ecommerce [worked through an 8% USPS rate increase](https://www.practicalecommerce.com/contribution-margin-guides-ecommerce-growth) and showed contribution margin on a $100 order falling from $25.00 to $24.44. Small changes per order add up across thousands of orders.

## A worked example with two products

This is how the spreadsheet shows which products are losing money in practice. The sample store below sells a jacket and a beanie.

| Line | Jacket | Beanie |
|---|---|---|
| Price | $60 | $25 |
| Units sold per month | 200 | 300 |
| Revenue | $12,000 | $7,500 |
| Variable cost per unit | $56 | $14 |
| Contribution per unit | $4 | $11 |
| Total contribution | $800 | $3,300 |

The jacket brings in 60% more revenue and earns less than a quarter of the beanie's contribution. Most of the jacket's cost is advertising. It needs $16 of ads per sale to keep selling, while the beanie sells through repeat customers and search. A revenue ranking would tell you to push the jacket harder, which makes the problem bigger.

## What a healthy contribution margin looks like

A common target for an online store is a contribution margin of about 20% of revenue after marketing. Eightx's [benchmarks by vertical](https://eightx.co/blog/average-contribution-margin-by-vertical) use 20% as the level where a brand covers its fixed costs and can grow, and report supplement brands between 20% and 35%. Products well below your store average are the ones to review first.

Those benchmarks come from a vendor and from brands with $5 million or more in revenue. A small store's fixed costs differ, so use them as a guide rather than a rule.

To see how many orders you need each month, divide your fixed costs by contribution per order. Polar Analytics [gives an example](https://www.polaranalytics.com/post/contribution-margin-ratio-per-unit) of $24,000 in monthly fixed costs and $24 of contribution per order. That store needs 1,000 orders a month to break even.

## What to do with the products at the bottom

Once you know which products are losing money, you have four options for each one: raise the price, cut its costs, cut its ad spend, or stop selling it. Start with the product that loses the most dollars in total, because fixing it adds the most profit. Then work up the list one product at a time.

Raising the price is often the fastest test. A small increase on a product with loyal buyers can turn a loss into a profit without any other change. Watch units sold for the next four weeks to check that demand holds.

Cutting costs means looking at packaging, shipping zones and supplier terms. Some products cost more to ship because of their size or weight. A different box or a free-shipping threshold can fix them.

Cutting ad spend works when a product only sells because you pay for every sale. Move that budget to products with a higher contribution per unit. Compare total contribution after a month.

Stopping a product is the last option, and sometimes the right one. If a product cannot cover its own variable costs at any price customers accept, each sale makes the business poorer. Some products bring in buyers who then purchase other items. Check whether its orders usually include other products before you drop it.

## FAQ

### What is the difference between gross margin and contribution margin?

Gross margin subtracts only the product cost from revenue. Contribution margin also subtracts shipping, payment fees, returns and ad spend. It shows what each sale adds toward rent, salaries and profit, so a product can have a healthy gross margin and still lose money on every order.

### How often should I check which products are losing money?

Once a month is enough for most small stores. Check again after any change in shipping rates, supplier prices or ad spend, because each of those moves contribution margin quickly.

### Do I need special software for this?

A spreadsheet plus exports from your store, payment provider and ad platforms is enough for a store with a few hundred products. Paid tools save time once you sell many products across several channels.

### Should I include fixed costs per product?

Leave fixed costs out of the product ranking. Rent and salaries stay the same whatever you sell, so splitting them across products makes the ranking less accurate. Compare products on contribution, then check that total contribution covers your fixed costs.
