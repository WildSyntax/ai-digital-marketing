## Script
### Hook (0:00–0:30)
Your dashboard shows forty charts, and none of them tells you what to do on Monday morning.
That is not a data problem. It is a too-many-numbers problem. In the next few minutes I will
give you nine numbers, one comparison, and a twenty-minute routine. By the end you will know
which part of your store moved last week, and what to do about it.

### Why weekly is the right rhythm
Let's start with timing. Daily numbers are noisy. One slow Tuesday tells you almost nothing,
and checking every morning invites panic over random swings. Monthly reviews have the
opposite flaw. By the time you see the drop, you have already lost four weeks of sales.
Weekly sits in the middle. Shero Commerce makes the same argument: weekly is the best balance
between monthly reviews that are too slow and daily figures that are too noisy. One rule keeps
the habit honest. Compare each week to the week before, not to the same week last year. Your
store has changed too much in twelve months for that older number to be a fair yardstick. A
full week also smooths out the weekend effect, so Monday to Sunday comparisons are fair.
If you only change one habit after this video, make it the fixed comparison, because it removes most of the guesswork.

### The nine KPIs worth your twenty minutes
Here is the list. One, revenue, compared with last week. It is the headline. Two, total
orders. Shero Commerce suggests that a week-over-week drop of twenty percent or more calls
for immediate investigation, while a dip of five to ten percent can be normal. Three, sessions
by channel: search, social, email, paid ads and direct. Four, conversion rate, the share of
sessions that end in a purchase. Five, average order value, which is revenue divided by
orders. Six, cart abandonment rate. If it jumps, check the shipping costs shown at checkout.
Seven, return on ad spend, if you run paid ads. Read it next to margin. Eight, returning
customer rate. Nine, fulfilment time, because slow shipping hurts retention a few weeks later.
Keep all nine in one sheet, so the whole review stays short.
Do not worry about perfect formulas on day one. A saved report from your store platform is enough to start.

### How to read the numbers together
One number rarely gives you the answer. The value comes from reading two or three side by
side. Here is a hypothetical. Revenue is down twelve percent and orders are down eleven
percent. Average order value barely moved, so the problem is the number of buyers. Next, check
sessions by channel. If paid traffic fell, your ad budget or ad approval is the likely cause.
If traffic held steady but conversion dropped, look at the site itself. Now a second case.
Revenue is flat, but profit feels worse. Check return on ad spend and shipping costs. Often a
store sells the same amount while paying more to win each order. For each number, ask one
question: what changed, and what is the most likely reason? Write the answer in one sentence.
Then pick one action, and move on. The goal is a decision, not a prettier chart.

### What to leave out of the weekly review
Some metrics are real, but they belong on a slower clock. Customer lifetime value needs months
of data to mean anything, so look at it once a quarter. Growth Suite advises measuring weekly
and comparing against your own trend, with daily checks only during campaigns. Then there are
vanity numbers: social followers, page views, email list size. They feel good, but they rarely
change what you do on Monday. Here is the test. If a number never leads to an action, remove
it from the sheet. Nine numbers fit on one screen, and a review you actually finish beats a
perfect one you skip. So keep the list short on purpose, and resist adding a tenth number just
because your platform offers it.
Fewer numbers means you will actually look at all of them.

### A simple weekly routine
Pick a fixed slot, such as Monday at nine. Then follow the same five steps every time. Step
one, open your saved report and copy the nine numbers into your sheet. Step two, add the
change versus last week for each one. Step three, flag anything that moved by more than ten
percent, using your own judgment as the store owner. Step four, write one sentence on the most
likely cause of each flag. Step five, choose one action and one owner for the week. Over a few
months, your sheet becomes a record of what normal looks like for your store. That record is
worth more than any benchmark, because your products, prices and customers are yours alone.
It also makes the next bad week much less scary.
And if you are still learning your reports, take the first few weeks slowly. The habit matters more than speed.

traffic, conversion, or order value. Next time your revenue moves, you will know where to look first, and that is most of the work. Finish with one action and one owner. If this helped,
